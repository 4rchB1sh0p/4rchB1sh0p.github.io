Playing Detective

Growth

Many find documentation dull, especially process documentation. But I have had some fantastic experiences with it, mostly because good documentation turns you into a quiet detective.

A few years ago, I documented processes for two bodhshalas with Bodh Shiksha Samiti, an NGO focused on educating underprivileged children in Rajasthan. Bodhshalas are community-driven schools, starting from land selection to construction, and every small decision has a story attached to it.

One of these schools even sparked the NGO's creation over 20 years ago. Together with a group of teachers, I documented two decades of their journey and learned how much careful observation can reveal. A wall is not just a wall when a village argued for it, funded it, built it, and then trusted it with its children.

The lesson stayed with me: when you write down how something works, you also discover what people value, what they protect, and what they quietly improve when nobody is making a slide deck about it.

It’s Complicated

Life

Living alone abroad has been a journey of self-discovery. Away from the familiar chaos of life, I found a unique peace in solitude, but it did not arrive in a cinematic way.

It came through ordinary things: learning which train door opens closest to the exit, cooking one sensible meal after a long day, walking through a city where nobody knows your default version.

The quiet moments, the freedom to explore new places, and the chance to truly understand myself brought a sense of calm I never knew I needed. Some days the silence felt generous. Some days it was heavy. Both were honest.

It is complicated, but in the silence of foreign lands, I found the tranquility that eluded me at home. Not perfect peace, exactly, but enough room to hear myself think.

Notes to My Younger Self

Growth

As we get older, we get wiser. Or at least we hope so. Even if we cannot turn all our lessons into perfect sayings, we do learn some useful things.

If I could chat with my younger self, just starting out in social work over ten years ago, I would remind him to stay curious, document carefully, and listen longer than feels comfortable.

I would tell him not to confuse urgency with importance. Some work needs speed, yes, but most meaningful work needs trust, and trust does not grow well under constant performance.

Those notes are still useful today: keep asking better questions, keep showing up, and let the work change you. The best parts of the work rarely announce themselves at the beginning.
